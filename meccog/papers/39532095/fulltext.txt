TITLE: Decreased lipidated ApoE-receptor interactions confer protection against pathogenicity of ApoE and its lipid cargoes in lysosomes

## ABSTRACT
SummaryWhile APOE is the strongest genetic modifier for late onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD), molecular mechanisms underlying isoform-dependent risk and relevance of ApoE-associated lipids remain elusive. Here, we report that impaired LDL receptor (LDLR) binding of lipidated ApoE2 (lipApoE2) avoids LDLR recycling defects observed with lipApoE3/E4 and decreases uptake of cholesteryl esters (CE), lipids linked to neurodegeneration. In human neurons, addition of ApoE carrying polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA)-CE revealed an allelic series (ApoE4>ApoE3>ApoE2) associated with lipofuscinosis, an age-related lysosomal pathology resulting from lipid peroxidation. Lipofuscin increased lysosomal accumulation of tau fibrils and was elevated in APOE4 mouse brain with exacerbation by tau pathology. Intrahippocampal injections of PUFA-CE-lipApoE4 was sufficient to induce lipofuscinosis in wild-type mice. Finally, the protective Christchurch mutation also reduced LDLR binding and phenocopied ApoE2. Collectively, our data strongly suggest decreased lipApoE-LDLR interactions minimize LOAD risk by reducing deleterious effects of endolysosomal targeting of ApoE and associated pathogenic lipids.

## INTRODUCTION
APOE genotype is the most significant genetic modifier for late onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD), with APOE4 and APOE2 alleles greatly increasing and decreasing disease risk, respectively, relative to the more common APOE3 allele1. Apolipoprotein E (ApoE) has been implicated in various cellular pathways linked to AD pathogenesis, including amyloid-β (Aβ) clearance and aggregation, tau pathology, metabolism, endolysosomal trafficking, and neuroinflammation1–3. Reducing ApoE levels is generally protective in AD models1,4–6.

ApoE is the most abundant apolipoprotein in the brain, where it is primarily expressed by astrocytes and reactive microglia7, regulating cholesterol metabolism8,9. In disease models, ApoE4 perturbs cholesterol regulation, including expression of cholesterol metabolism-related genes, cholesterol transport and esterification10–13. Together with studies showing cholesterol dysregulation in a range of AD models and patients’ brains, these studies suggest lipids are central to AD pathogenesis14–18. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying ApoE isoform-dependent lipid dyshomeostasis in LOAD are poorly understood.

One of the most striking ApoE isoform-specific biochemical features is that ApoE2 exhibits defective binding to low density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), based on ligand competition assays19,20. The amino acid substitution R158C, which distinguishes ApoE2 from ApoE3 and ApoE4, disrupts LDLR interaction in the lipidated state19,21. While this feature may relate to type III hyperlipoproteinemia observed in 1–10% of APOE2 homozygotes22, it has been underappreciated as a potential protective mechanism for LOAD, despite the fact LDLR is a key receptor for lipidated ApoE (lipApoE) in the brain23. Recently, a study reported a homozygous APOE3 Christchurch mutation (R136S) carrier presenting a ~30-year delay in dementia onset caused by the PSEN1 E280A mutation and limited tau pathology despite abundant amyloid burden24. A mildly protective effect of the heterozygous Christchurch mutation was also found in the same kindred25. The homozygous Christchurch mutation also suppresses AD pathologies in amyloid and non-amyloid mouse models26,27. While the protective mechanism of the R136S variant has been attributed to reduced binding to heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs)24, it is unclear if it involves the lipidated state of ApoE. Additionally, this mutation was also reported to decrease LDLR binding by ~60%28. This commonality between the Christchurch mutation and ApoE2 led us to speculate that reduced lipApoE binding to LDLR could be a mechanism underlying decreased AD risk.

In this study, we demonstrate impaired LDLR binding of lipApoE2 results in decreased LDLR-mediated uptake of ApoE and associated lipids, including CE, thereby preventing excessive activation of microglia. Moreover, ApoE carrying polyunsaturated CE revealed an allelic series of neuronal lipofuscin accumulation (ApoE4 > ApoE3 > ApoE2) caused by lysosomal lipid peroxidation and accompanied by differential accumulation of tau fibrils in human iPSC-derived neurons, with ApoE4 phenotypes rescuable by a mutation impairing receptor binding. ApoE4-potentiated lipofuscinosis was also observed in vulnerable brain regions of a tauopathy mouse model. Together with findings that Christchurch variant largely phenocopies ApoE2, our study suggests decreased lipidated ApoE-receptor interaction is a key protective mechanism for AD.

## RESULTS
#### Cellular uptake of ApoE by LDLR is isoform-dependent
LDLR is a key receptor for lipApoE. Analysis of single-nucleus RNA-seq data from the Religious Orders Study and Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP)29 showed broad LDLR expression in various cell types in human brains, especially in excitatory neurons, where its mRNA levels were comparable to other ApoE receptors such as LRP1, LRP1B and LRP8/APOER2 (Figure S1A). Since lipidation of ApoE is required to expose its LDLR-binding site30, we lipidated HEK293 cell-derived ApoE (lipApoE) with 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC). Formation of homogeneous ApoE nanodiscs with a 10–20 nm diameter was confirmed by negative stain transmission electron microscopy, with no appreciable isoform-specific differences (Figure S1B). Previously, impaired lipApoE2-LDLR binding was inferred from competitive LDL-binding assays, where LDLR binding affinities of ApoE isoforms inversely correlated with LDL binding19,20. To directly assess ApoE-LDLR binding, we developed a cell-based binding assay, in which lipApoE was labeled by FITC and incubated with 293T cells overexpressing human LDLR for 1 h at 4°C, a temperature that prevents endocytosis. Robust cell surface signals were observed for lipApoE3 and lipApoE4, while minimal signals were observed for lipApoE2, indicating reduced binding of lipApoE2 to LDLR (Figure 1A–B).

Next, homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence (HTRF) and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) were utilized to quantitatively measure binding of lipApoE to the extracellular domain (ECD) of LDLR. With HTRF, minimal LDLR binding was detected for lipApoE2 at concentrations up to 100 nM (Figure 1C). Conversely, lipApoE3 and lipApoE4 both exhibited high affinity binding, demonstrating a half maximum effective concentration (EC50) of ~13 nM. SPR similarly revealed a high affinity LDLR binding for both lipApoE4 and lipApoE3 (KD = 9 nM and 16 nM, respectively), whereas the lipApoE2-LDLR affinity was ~50-fold lower than lipApoE3 (KD = 800 nM; Figure 1D).

To assess impact of LDLR binding on lipApoE internalization, cells were treated with lipApoE labeled with pHrodo, a pH-sensitive dye that fluoresces in acidic endolysosomal compartments. Compared to lipApoE3 and lipApoE4, lipApoE2 showed significantly lower pHrodo signals after 5–6 h or 1 d incubation in wildtype (WT) HAP1 cells, indicating decreased cellular uptake (Figure S1C–D, Figure 1E–F). While both LDLR KO and LRP1 KO HAP1 cells demonstrated partially impaired uptake of lipApoE at 5–6 h, only LDLR KO cells showed dramatically reduced pHrodo signals for lipApoE3/E4 at 1 d. Reduced lipApoE2 internalization was also observed in H4 neuroglioma cells (Figure S1E–F, Figure 1G–H). Notably, co-incubation with LDLR ECD, which competes with cell surface LDLR for ApoE binding, suppressed internalization of lipApoE3/E4 both at 5–6 h and 1 d. Co-treatment with heparin, which competes with cell surface HSPGs for ApoE binding, only produced a significant inhibitory effect at 5–6 h. Co-incubation with the ECD of control receptor CD98hc did not affect lipApoE uptake (Figure S1G–H). Therefore, while multiple receptors mediate lipApoE internalization, LDLR plays a dominant role with longer-term treatment. Lastly, in human APOE KO iPSC-derived astrocytes (iAstrocytes), pHrodo-lipApoE uptake showed similar isoform trend that was abolished by blocking uptake of lipApoE3/E4 with LDLR ECD (Figure S1I–J), validating findings in a CNS cell type.

#### LipApoE3/E4 alter cellular distribution of LDLR and impair LDL uptake
To explore if LDLR-mediated uptake of lipApoE impacts LDLR recycling, we immunostained cell surface LDLR after 1 d lipApoE treatment. In H4, WT HAP1, and APOE KO iAstrocytes, incubation with lipApoE3/E4 significantly reduced surface LDLR relative to lipApoE2, revealing impaired LDLR recycling accompanying increased LDLR-mediated lipApoE uptake (Figure 2A–D, Figure S2A–B). Interestingly, lipApoE2 raised surface LDLR in H4 and, more prominently, APOE KO iAstrocytes (Figure 2A–D). Gene expression analysis of APOE KO iAstrocytes revealed upregulation of LDLR as well as enzymes involved in cholesterol synthesis (HMGCR and DHCR7) upon treatment with all lipApoE isoforms (Figure S2C), suggesting potential homeostatic responses to increased cholesterol efflux mediated by lipApoE. While all 3 ApoE isoforms impacted LDLR mRNA levels, lipApoE3/E4 abrogated the expected increase in surface LDLR by impairing its recycling. The compensatory upregulation of LDLR expression was most pronounced in astrocytes as this cell type is equipped with efficient cholesterol efflux machinery31.

Immunostaining of total LDLR (cell surface + intracellular) in H4 cells after lipApoE3/E4 treatment showed a modest but significant reduction in signals, which also appeared slightly more clustered (Figure 2E–F). Cell-associated ApoE immunoreactivity extensively colocalized with LDLR puncta (Figure 2G) outside of transferrin-labeled recycling endosomes (Figure S2D), suggesting “trapping” of LDLR by ApoE in other endolysosomal compartments. In contrast, H4 cells treated with DiI-conjugated LDL (DiI-LDL) showed minimal colocalization of DiI-LDL with LDLR (Figure S2E) but significant overlap with LAMP1 immunoreactivity (Figure S2F), consistent with rapid endosomal dissociation of LDLR from LDL, with the latter targeted to lysosomes.

Super-resolution imaging on APOE KO iAstrocytes confirmed internalized lipApoE completely colocalized with endogenous LDLR (Mander’s coefficient for ApoE puncta with colocalizing LDLR = 1 for all 3 isoforms), largely inside LAMP1 compartments (Figure 2H) (Mander’s coefficients for ApoE puncta with colocalizing LAMP1 = 0.823 ± 0.061 for ApoE2; 0.903 ± 0.024 for ApoE3; 0.900 ± 0.008 for ApoE4). ApoE isoform differences were manifested in the differential levels of internalized lipApoE (ApoE4 > ApoE3 > ApoE2), giving rise to the same isoform trend for LAMP1-positive vesicles containing ApoE immunoreactivity (Mander’s coefficients = 0.266 ± 0.045 for ApoE2, 0.410 ± 0.049 for ApoE3, 0.497 ± 0.090 for ApoE4) (Figure 2I).

Next, we performed DiI-LDL uptake in serum-starved H4 cells. When cells were co-treated with lipApoE and DiI-LDL for 3–4 h, both lipApoE3 and lipApoE4 led to 60–65% inhibition of DiI-LDL uptake, likely via a direct competition with LDL for LDLR binding, whereas lipApoE2 had no effect (Figure 2J, L). When DiI-LDL was spiked into media after 1d pre-treatment with lipApoE3/E4, DiI-LDL uptake was nearly abolished (Figure 2K, L). In contrast, lipApoE2 pre-treatment modestly enhanced DiI-LDL uptake, consistent with slightly increased LDLR levels induced by ApoE2 (Figure 2B, F). Taken together, increased LDLR-mediated uptake of lipApoE3/E4, coupled with inefficient dissociation of LDLR from lipApoE upon internalization, results in redistribution of cell surface LDLR to endolysosomes, which exacerbates the suppression of LDL uptake by competitive binding.

#### ApoE isoform- and LDLR-dependent lipid uptake impacts lipid homeostasis
As CNS lipoproteins are HDL-like16, we next tested if ApoE isoforms differentially mediate cellular uptake of pHrodo-labeled HDL pre-complexed with non-lipidated ApoE before addition to serum-starved H4 cells. With no ApoE, minimal uptake of pHrodo-HDL was observed for up to 1 d (Figure 3A, C), since HDL does not bind LDLR. Pre-complexing pHrodo-HDL with ApoE isoforms promoted its uptake in the order of ApoE4 ~ ApoE3 > ApoE2 (Figure 3A, C). LDLR ECD suppressed pHrodo-HDL uptake with ApoE3 or ApoE4, indicating a key role of LDLR in ApoE-mediated HDL internalization. Blocking ApoE-HSPG interaction with heparin also attenuated pHrodo-HDL uptake with more pronounced effects at 6 h than at 1 d (Figure S3A–C), suggesting HSPGs may function as co-receptors to facilitate engagement with LDLR and/or LRP1, as shown for peripheral apolipoproteins32. Interestingly, the low pHrodo-HDL uptake mediated by ApoE2 was blocked by heparin while minimally inhibited by LDLR ECD, suggesting shifted dependency to HSPGs for ApoE2 due to impaired LDLR binding (Figure 3C, Figure S3B, C).

Elevated CE levels have been reported in AD brains as well as AD models12,14,15. To explore if ApoE isoforms can differentially modulate CE uptake, we pre-complexed BODIPYconjugated CE (BODIPY-CE) with HDL in the presence or absence of ApoE, then treated H4 cells with the complex. Without ApoE, HDL largely prevented cellular uptake of BODIPY-CE (Figure S3D, F). Pre-complexing BODIPY-CE/HDL with ApoE3 or ApoE4, but not ApoE2, produced higher intracellular BODIPY signals, suggesting ApoE3/E4 promotes internalization of BODIPY-CE via HDL, which otherwise sequesters BODIPY-CE extracellularly. The increased BODIPY-CE uptake mediated by ApoE3/E4 was abrogated by LDLR ECD, indicating LDLR dependency. Interestingly, a subset of BODIPY signals appeared as cytoplasmic puncta that colocalized with PLIN2, a lipid droplet marker (Figure S3E), and mirrored total BODIPY signals (Figure S3G). Since CE is hydrolyzed into cholesterol and fatty acid in lysosomes, and BODIPY is conjugated to the fatty acyl group of CE, BODIPY puncta likely represent BODIPY-fatty acids incorporated into lipid droplets. ApoE isoform- and LDLR-dependent BODIPY-CE uptake was similarly observed in APOE KO iAstrocytes (Figure 3B, D).

To determine how differential CE uptake impacts cholesterol homeostasis transcriptionally in H4 cells and APOE KO iAstrocytes, we used qPCR. In both cell types, ApoE3/E4 treatment, which caused higher CE uptake, upregulated expression of ABCA1 and ABCG1, genes involved in cholesterol efflux, while genes mediating cholesterol synthesis (HMGCR, SREBF2 and DHCR7) were downregulated, as was LDLR (Figure 3E, Figure S3H). These transcriptional changes likely reflect homeostatic responses to increased cholesterol burden. LDLR ECD attenuated ApoE3/E4-induced transcriptional alterations, as expected from blocked CE uptake.

Next, lipidomic analysis was performed on APOE KO iAstrocytes after 1 d treatment with CE (18:1)/HDL/ApoE complex (Figure 3F–G, Figure S3I). A heatmap for the top 25 lipid changes revealed segregation by ApoE isoforms, with ApoE2- and ApoE4-treated cells most distinct from each other while ApoE3-treated ones being intermediate (Figure 3F). The most prominent differences were observed for various CE species, phosphatidylcholine (PC) or phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) with long chains of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), and multiple triglycerides (TG), which exhibited a trend of ApoE4 ≥ ApoE3 > ApoE2. LDLR ECD co-treatment rescued ApoE4 phenotypes (Figure 3G, Figure S3I). CE(18:1) was significantly elevated with ApoE3 or ApoE4 treatment compared to ApoE2, mirroring isoform-dependent uptake of exogenously supplied CE. Similar changes were observed in several other CE species, potentially from CE carried by HDL and/or CE generated from Acetyl-Coenzyme A acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1)-mediated re-esterification of free cholesterol derived from lysosomal hydrolysis of CE(18:1), like CE(16:1). Notably, most CE species were lowered with ApoE2 treatment compared to the no-ApoE control, suggesting the cholesterol-efflux role of ApoE2 outweighs its lipid uptake function, leading to an overall reduction in cholesterol burden.

#### Differential lipid burden modulates inflammatory responses and transcription in microglia
Lipid challenges, including cholesterol, can induce pro-inflammatory responses in microglia8,9,33. To test if ApoE isoform-dependent lipid burden differentially impacts inflammatory responses in human iPSC-derived microglia (iMg), we applied the BODIPY-CE/HDL/ApoE treatment paradigm to APOE KO iMg in the presence of LPS, a pro-inflammatory stimulus. Interestingly, LPS upregulated LDLR expression (Figure 4C, LDLR panel; Figure S4A), which increased baseline uptake of BODIPY-CE in the absence of ApoE (Figure 4A–B). Treatment of LPS-stimulated APOE KO iMg with ApoE3/E4 enhanced BODIPY-CE uptake relative to ApoE2 and LDLR ECD completely reversed the effect of ApoE4 (Figure 4A–B). Transcriptional changes in cholesterol homeostasis were consistent with those seen in the other cell types (Figure 4C, compared to Figure 3E and Figure S3H). BODIPY-CE uptake results were replicated using APOE KO iMg from a different round of differentiation (Figure S4B–C). Cytokine/chemokine measurements on conditioned media collected at 1 d revealed a trend of ApoE4 > ApoE3 > ApoE2 ~ ‘ApoE4 + LDLR ECD’ for multiple cytokines, including IL-9, IL-13, IL-18 and IFN-a2 (Figure 4D), suggesting increased lipid burden indeed promotes inflammatory responses. Despite a similar CE uptake induced by ApoE3 and ApoE4, the latter caused significantly higher secretion of the above-mentioned cytokines. A subset of cytokines showing ApoE4-specific changes were not rescuable by LDLR ECD, potentially implicating mechanisms other than lipid uptake (Figure S4D).

Next, we performed RNA-seq on the same cells and differential expression analysis for individual genes revealed few differences between ApoE4 and ApoE3 treatment (Figure S4E). Moreover, top genes showing the most significant differences among ApoE4 vs. ApoE2, ApoE3 vs. ApoE2 and ApoE4 vs. ‘ApoE4 + LDLR ECD’ comparisons were enriched with those involved in cholesterol homeostasis (Figure 4E, Figure S4E). Additional pathways significantly modulated by ApoE isoforms included oxidative phosphorylation, eukaryotic translation elongation/initiation and interferon response (Figure 4F, Figure S4F). In general, ApoE3/E4 treatment increased gene expression in these pathways (Figure S4G–H), pointing to upregulation of protein synthesis and energy expenditure upon increased lipid burden. Compared to ApoE3 treatment, ApoE4 treatment more weakly upregulated the oxidative phosphorylation and the interferon response pathways, which is opposite to what would be expected from the cytokine protein data (Figure 4F, Figure S4F). Lastly, LDLR ECD prevented the ApoE4-induced lipid burden and largely reverted gene expression changes observed with ApoE4, although several pathways showed exacerbated ApoE4 effects (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation and mitotic spindle in Figure 4F), possibly due to effects related to blocking ApoE-independent LDLR functions.

#### ApoE isoforms carrying CE(20:4) differentially induce lipofuscin in cultured cells
Our earlier study found PUFA-containing CE, including CE(20:4), prominently increased in the microglia of APOE4 KI/P301S MAPT mice10. Given that CE(20:4) is one of the most abundant CE species in human plasma34, we measured CE(20:4) levels in human CSF samples and detected elevated CE(20:4) in AD patients compared to age-matched control (Figure S5A). Since PUFAs are peroxidation-prone and may facilitate the formation of neuronal lipofuscin, a lysosomal pathology that is a hallmark of aging35, we generated ApoE particles consisting of POPC and CE(20:4) (CE(20:4)-lipApoE) (Figure S5B–C) and tested if ApoE isoforms can differentially modulate lipofuscin accumulation in human iPSC-derived neurons (iNeurons) (Figure S5D). Likely reflecting variable incorporation of CE(20:4) into the core, CE(20:4)-lipApoE particles appeared more heterogeneous than POPC-lipApoE, with diameters ranging from 10 to 40 nm, albeit with no appreciable isoform differences (Figure S5C, compared with Figure S1B). Longitudinal live imaging of CE(20:4)-lipApoE-treated iNeurons revealed both ApoE isoform-dependent and time-dependent accumulation of autofluorescence indicative of lipofuscin (Figure S5E–F, Figure 5A–B). At 7 d, lipofuscin induced by CE(20:4)-lipApoE3/E4 was weakly detectable, while signals for CE(20:4)-ApoE2 were minimal. After longer incubation (14 d and 21 d), a progressively larger difference between ApoE4 and ApoE3 emerged (Figure S5G, Figure 5B). Lipofuscin appeared mostly perikaryal and localized in LAMP1-positive lysosomes shown by super-resolution imaging (Figure 5C). Perikaryal immunoreactivities for 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), a major peroxidation product of ω−6 fatty acids like arachidonic acid (20:4), also followed the order ApoE4 > ApoE3 > ApoE2 (Figure 5D–E), supporting the role of lipid peroxidation in lipofuscin biogenesis. To assess the impact of blocking receptor-mediated ApoE4 uptake, we exploited the K146E mutation, which impairs both LDLR and HSPG binding36. CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 carrying the K146E mutation greatly diminished lipofuscin and 4-HNE signals compared to ApoE4 (Figure S5E–G, Figure 5A–B and D–E), confirming lipofuscin formation requires CE(20:4)-lipApoE internalization. LDLR ECD co-treatment with CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 dramatically reduced lipofuscin (Figure 5F–G), confirming LDLR dependency. ApoE isoform-dependent lipofuscin accumulation was replicated in WT H4 and HAP1 cells, similarly blocked by LDLR ECD or KO of LDLR, but not LRP1 (Figure 5H–I, Figure S5H–I).

We next used H4 cells to further understand mechanisms underlying lipofuscinosis. Replacing CE(20:4) with CE(18:1) in the POPC-ApoE4 lipoproteins abolished lipofuscin induction (Figure 5J–K), denoting a PUFA requirement. Pre-complexing CE(20:4)-lipApoE3 with Vitamin E, a lipophilic anti-oxidant, significantly decreased lipofuscin (Figure 5L–M), indicating lipid peroxidation requirement. Co-treatment of CE(20:4)-lipApoE3 with Lalistat-2, an inhibitor of lysosomal acid lipase that induced buildup of CE species, including CE(20:4) (Figure S5J), significantly enhanced lipofuscin formation (Figure 5L and N), suggesting lipofuscinosis involves peroxidation of PUFA in intact CE rather than free PUFA. Intriguingly, while Lalistat-2 treatment alone increased cellular levels of CE(20:4) to a similar extent as CE(20:4)-lipApoE3 (Figure S5J), Lalistat-2 alone did not induce lipofuscin (Figure 5N), revealing a synergistic effect between PUFA-CE and ApoE for lipofuscin formation.

Given similar LDLR-binding by lipApoE3 and lipApoE4, other properties may contribute to the ApoE4 vs. ApoE3 difference in lipofuscinosis. To test if differential aggregation of CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 compared to CE(20:4)-lipApoE3 may play a role, we assessed aggregation status of different isoforms of CE(20:4)-lipApoE after 4 d incubation at 37°C with ultracentrifugation37. CE(20:4)-lipApoE remained largely soluble and mostly recovered in the supernatant fraction upon incubation at pH 7.4, but abundant protein was found in the pellet fraction indicative of aggregation after incubation at pH 4.5, which mimics lysosomal pH (Figure 5O–P). Interestingly, CE(20:4)-ApoE3 demonstrated the lowest aggregation at both pH 7.4 and pH 4.5, while CE(20:4)-ApoE2 exhibited the highest aggregation at pH 7.4 but comparable aggregation as CE(20:4)-ApoE4 at pH 4.5 (Figure 5O–Q, Figure S5K). Despite variability in the extent of aggregation across two independent batches of lipidated ApoE, the pH- and ApoE isoform-dependent aggregation of CE(20:4)-lipApoE was conserved. These results suggest internalization of CE(20:4)-lipApoE into acidic endolysosomal compartments likely promotes its aggregation, but the impaired cellular uptake of lipApoE2 protects against this deleterious event. The lower aggregation propensity of CE(20:4)-lipApoE3 may underlie its attenuated lipofuscin induction compared to CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 despite similar endolysosomal accumulation. In addition, POPC-lipApoE without CE(20:4) and CE(18:1)-lip ApoE4 that does not contain peroxidation-prone PUFA-CE showed minimal aggregation upon 37°C incubation at pH 4.5 (Figure S5L–M), suggesting PUFA-CE increases the propensity of ApoE particles to aggregate.

#### PUFA-CE-ApoE4 promotes lipofuscin deposits in vivo
To explore if ApoE isoform-dependent lipofuscinosis occurs in vivo, we examined autofluorescent signals in the brains of ~9.5-month-old APOE KO (EKO), APOE3 KI (E3), and APOE4 KI (E4) mice with and without P301S MAPT background. Overall, lipofuscin was most frequently detected in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, the most severely degenerated brain regions in P301S MAPT/APOE KI mice (Figure 6A–B, Figure S6A–B). Human ApoE4 expression alone significantly enhanced lipofuscin buildup in the hippocampus relative to ApoE3 (Figure 6A–B). While P301S MAPT expression generally promoted lipofuscinosis across APOE genotypes, P301S MAPT/APOE4 KI mice showed the highest lipofuscin in both brain regions, suggesting ApoE4 and mutant tau together exacerbate lipofuscinosis. Co-labeling with neuronal and microglial markers (NeuN and Iba1, respectively) revealed predominant localization of lipofuscin in neurons with occasional detection in Tau/E4 microglia (Figure 6C). These findings demonstrate ApoE4-potentiated lipofuscinosis in vivo.

Our recent study demonstrated LXR agonist dosing significantly decreased PUFA-CE species [CE(18:2), CE(20:4), CE(20:5) and CE(22:6)] that accumulated in Tau/E4 mouse brain, accompanied by reduced tau pathology and neurodegeneration10. LXR agonist also significantly reduced lipofuscin in Tau/E4 hippocampi in the same cohort (Figure 6D–E), supporting a correlation between PUFA-CE accumulation and lipofuscinosis. To assess the sufficiency of PUFA-CE-containing ApoE4 lipoproteins in causing lipofuscinosis in vivo, we performed stereotaxic injection of PBS or CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 into each side of the hippocampus of 6-month-old WT mice (Figure 6F–G). Although lipofuscin was observed along the injection path, likely reflecting parenchymal damage, more lipofuscin was detected in regions throughout the hemisphere receiving CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 inoculation than that receiving PBS. This difference appeared most prominent in cortex, hippocampus and fimbria-fornix, and away from injection paths. Furthermore, co-labeling with neuronal and microglial markers near the injection site in the cortex and hippocampus revealed lipofuscin in both cell types but significantly more in microglia than in neurons (Figure S6C–D), perhaps reflecting microglial activation caused by injection-induced physical damage. These results demonstrate PUFA-CE-lipApoE4 is sufficient to promote lipofuscinosis in living WT animals.

#### PUFA-CE-ApoE-induced lipofuscin perturbs lipid metabolism and lysosomal function
Given robust lipidomic and lipofuscin phenotypes in Tau/E4 mice, we investigated if CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 impacts lipids in iNeurons overexpressing P301S tau. Lipidomics uncovered significant alterations in numerous lipid species after 14 d treatment with CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 compared to the other isoforms (Figure S6E–F). Apart from the dramatic increase in CE(20:4) itself, multiple PUFA-containing TGs (e.g.,TG(20:4_34:2)) and plasmalogens (e.g., PE(P-16:0/20:4)) along with PE species, the precursor to plasmalogens, were significantly elevated. In contrast, several diglycerides (DG) and subgroups of sphingolipids, including sphingomyelins (SM), ceramides (Cer), and hexosylceramides (HexCer), showed significant reduction, demonstrating broad lipid dysregulation accompanies ApoE4-potentiated lipofuscinosis.

Increasing evidence suggests ApoE4 promotes spreading of tau pathology in AD brains38,39. To explore if ApoE4-potentiated lipofuscin influences neuronal accumulation of preformed tau fibrils (tau pffs), we added pHrodo-labeled tau pffs to iNeurons pre-treated with CE(20:4)-lipApoE for 14 d. After another 13–14 d incubation, total pHrodo signals from tau pffs that were internalized and accumulated in the endolysosomes demonstrated a trend of ApoE4 > ApoE3 > ApoE2 > K146E ApoE4, with the isoform-dependent difference being more pronounced among lipofuscin-positive neurons (Figure 6H–I), suggesting neuronal accumulation of tau pffs is augmented by a higher lipofuscin load. To confirm the effect of PUFA-CE-lipApoE4 on tau pff accumulation is causally linked to lipofuscin, we directly compared CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 with CE(18:1)-lipApoE4, which did not induce lipofuscin as CE(18:1) is not prone to peroxidation (Figure 6J–K). Indeed, CE(18:1)-lipApoE4 produced significantly lower accumulation of pHrodo-tau pffs than CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 (Figure 6L–M), consistent with lipofuscin-potentiated tau pff accumulation.

Next, we tested if lipofuscin produces lysosomal abnormalities in H4 cells. Cathepsin activity measured by the 6QC probe40 showed a modest but significant reduction with CE(20:4)-lipApoE3 or CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 treatment compared to other treatments that minimally induced lipofuscin (Figure S6G–H). LAMP1 immunostaining revealed no treatment effect on the total lysosomal area, but CE(20:4)-lipApoE3/E4 treatment resulted in prominent lysosomal clustering (Figure S6I–K), a phenotype previously observed in association with lysosomal impairment41,42. Therefore, acute lipofuscin induction by CE(20:4)-ApoE3/4 is sufficient to cause lysosomal defects.

#### Christchurch mutation R136S reduces LDLR binding and decreases lipid burden
Similar to ApoE2, Christchurch (R136S) mutation is protective in autosomal dominant AD cases24,25 and AD mouse models26,27. To understand if the R136S mutation exerts its protection partly via an LDLR-dependent manner, we introduced it into both ApoE3 and ApoE4, including the K146E ApoE4 variant as a positive control for receptor binding deficiency. Consistent with the literature24, HPLC-based heparin binding assay performed on non-lipidated ApoE demonstrated impaired heparin binding by the Christchurch mutation on both ApoE3 and ApoE4 background with the overall ranking of ApoE4 > ApoE3 > ApoE4ch > ApoE2 > ApoE3ch > K146E ApoE4 (Figure S7A). POPC-lipidated ApoE particles were generated for both ApoE3ch and ApoE4ch, with the size and morphology of POPC-lipApoE3ch nanodiscs confirmed to be similar to POPC-lipApoE2/E3/E4 by negative stain EM (Figure S7B, compared to Figure S1B). A competitive HTRF assay measuring binding between His-tagged lipApoE4 and biotinylated LDLR ECD in the presence of different concentrations of untagged lipApoE variants revealed a complete lack of inhibition by lipK146E ApoE4, our positive control, and greatly right-shifted inhibition curves of lipApoE3ch and lipApoE4ch compared to lipApoE3 and lipApoE4, respectively (Figure 7A), confirming decreased LDLR binding affinity by R136S mutation. Additionally, ApoE3ch and ApoE4ch showed significant reduction in pHrodo-lipApoE, pHrodo-HDL and BODIPY-CE uptake assays compared to their respective WT counterparts (Figure 7B–E, Figure S7C–D). Lastly, CE(20:4)-lipidated Christchurch mutants showed significantly attenuated lipofuscinosis (Figure 7F–G). Taken together, decreased LDLR-dependent cellular uptake of ApoE and its lipid cargoes may be a common mechanism underlying the protective effects of ApoE2 and ApoE3ch.

## DISCUSSION
Our study suggests impaired binding of lipApoE2 and Christchurch variant to LDLR may be protective in AD. Pertinent to ApoE’s physiological function of lipid transport, reduced LDLR binding by lipApoE2 lowered its cellular uptake and that of its lipid cargoes, preventing excessive intracellular lipid burden observed with lipApoE3/E4. Specifically, when ApoE carries PUFA-CE, LDLR-dependent internalization caused lysosomal deposition of lipofuscin, a pathological autofluorescent lipopigment of obscure origin since its discovery in 1840 (reviewed in ref.43) that relates to neuronal aging35,43 and vulnerability in AD brain44. PUFA-CE-lipApoE-induced lipofuscinosis mirrored the ApoE2 < ApoE3 < ApoE4 allelic series, involved lipid peroxidation of PUFA-CE and perturbed lysosomes, with an impact on lysosomal accumulation of tau pffs. Lipofuscinosis was also observed with endogenous expression of APOE in APOE4 KI mouse brains, where it was worsened by P301S MAPT expression and ameliorated by an LXR agonist previously shown to reduce brain PUFA-CE10. PUFA-CE also enhanced ApoE aggregation in an isoform-dependent manner, especially at acidic/lysosomal pH. Our study provides a mechanistic framework potentially clarifying ApoE isoform-dependent impact on LOAD risk, implicating receptor-mediated internalization of ApoE and associated pathogenic lipid cargoes.

Past studies have historically focused more on pathogenic mechanisms driven by ApoE4 than protective mechanisms mediated by ApoE21,21, which is also associated with greater longevity independently of AD45,46. To assess if reduced LDLR binding of lipApoE2 could be protective, we adopted a simple paradigm of treating cultured cells with lipid-complexed HEK293-purified ApoE isoforms. Using quantitative, direct binding assays, we demonstrated a ~50–100-fold decrease in LDLR-binding affinity for ApoE2 vs. ApoE3/E4 and a comparable binding affinity for ApoE3 and ApoE4. Human CSF ApoE concentrations were reported to be ~7–9 μg/ml (~206–265 nM), which are below the LDLR-binding KD of lipApoE2, i.e., ~800nM47,48, suggesting lipApoE2 minimally engages LDLR in the brain, consistent with Ldlr KO mouse studies23.

Previous work showed ApoE isoforms differentially perturb endosomal recycling of receptors, such as LRP8/ApoER2, with a deleterious impact of ApoE4 on signaling by reelin, a key ligand of this receptor49. Our study now demonstrates lipApoE isoform-dependent impact on the endolysosomal trafficking of a distinct ApoE receptor, LDLR, which primarily mediates lipid metabolism50. We first showed LDLR is the dominant receptor mediating uptake and endolysosomal accumulation of lipApoE in multiple human cellular models. LDLR-mediated uptake of lipApoE3 and lipApoE4 led to endolysosomal trapping of LDLR and depletion of cell surface LDLR, resulting in further reduced LDL uptake. LipApoE2 did not show these effects due to defective LDLR binding. Our findings thus potentially explain the reported APOE genotype effects on human plasma LDL levels, with ApoE2 and Christchurch variant associated with lower LDL51 or ApoB levels52, a profile that correlates with lower cardiovascular disease risk53. While LDL biology is mostly relevant for peripheral diseases32,54,55, the CNS primarily contains HDL-like particles, many of which harbors ApoE and can thus engage LDLR56. LDLR’s ligands in the CNS, besides ApoE, are not well defined. However, important roles were shown for LDLR in modulating AD pathologies57–59, in part via uptake and clearance of Aβ by astrocytes60. Therefore, reduced LDLR binding of ApoE2 may free up LDLR for binding and clearance of other ligands, including Aβ, thus decreasing amyloid pathology.

Growing evidence suggests ApoE’s role in lipid transport is connected to LOAD pathogenesis. ApoE-mediated transport of lipids from neurons to glia in flies61 and mammalian cells is neuroprotective62. Additionally, APOE4 expression in human iMG was shown to induce secretion of lipids, which promoted tau phosphorylation in neurons63. CE, the main transport and storage form of cholesterol, is of particular interest as a candidate disease-relevant ApoE cargo, based on its upregulation in AD patient brain and various AD models, along with the fact that its downregulation is protective in various AD models10,12,15,64. Our study showed in multiple cell types greatly enhanced CE uptake by HDL-complexed ApoE3 or ApoE4 compared to ApoE2 due to differential LDLR binding. While LDLR-dependent lipApoE uptake is a normal physiological process, we hypothesized the disease-relevant protective mechanism for ApoE2 involves decreased endolysosomal delivery of ApoE and pathogenic lipids into neurons. Of note, neurons are vulnerable to toxic lipids, such as oxidized lipids65,66, particularly when lysosomes are compromised67. Therefore, we assessed the pathogenic potential of peroxidation-prone PUFA-CE by treating iNeurons with CE(20:4)-lipApoE isoforms. We observed accumulation of lipofuscin along with an established toxic product of arachidonic acid peroxidation, 4-HNE, following the allelic series of ApoE4 > ApoE3 > ApoE2. Lipofuscin is believed to arise from oxidative damage and crosslinking of macromolecules induced by PUFA peroxidation35,43. Consistently, we found reduced lipofuscin accumulation with anti-oxidant Vitamin E and a complete lack of lipofuscinosis when CE(20:4) is replaced by peroxidation-resistant CE(18:1), supporting the role of lipid peroxidation in lipofuscinosis. Inhibition of lysosomal acid lipase, which broadly increased levels of CE including CE(20:4), exacerbated lipofuscinosis, but only in the presence of lipApoE. Importantly, lipofuscin reduced lysosomal proteolysis and caused abnormal lysosomal clustering, suggesting deleterious impact on lysosomes. Reducing ApoE4-receptor interactions with the K146E mutation rescued lipofuscin, 4-HNE and lysosomal phenotypes to levels observed with ApoE2.

While lipofuscin is key pathology in neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis and GRN-linked frontotemporal dementia, it may be more broadly involved in neurodegeneration68–71. Indeed, long and poorly myelinated cortical neurons with higher lipofuscin burden in the transentorhinal cortex are the most vulnerable to neurofibrillary tangle formation, and lipofuscin-free neurons are generally more resilient in AD44. Our findings linking ApoE isoform-dependent lipofuscinosis to tau pff accumulation thus offer a potential mechanism for the ApoE4 effect on enhancing spreading of tau pathology38,39. ApoE4-potentiated lipofuscin buildup was validated in APOE4 KI/P301S MAPT mouse brains, where it was observed in both neurons and microglia of vulnerable regions, namely the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex. Critically, chronic dosing of these mice with an LXR agonist previously shown to decrease PUFA-CE, including CE(20:4)10, significantly diminished lipofuscinosis. Moreover, intrahippocampal injections of CE(20:4)-lipApoE4 was sufficient to induce lipofuscin in WT mouse brain. Therefore, PUFA-CE species, together with ApoE, may be key drivers of brain lipofuscinosis. However, other lipids and proteins, such as ApoE receptors themselves given their sensitivity to peroxidation-derived reactive species72, may also be involved.

Besides characterizing the common protective variant ApoE2, we studied the rare ApoE R136S (Christchurch) variant, which is protective in the PSEN1 E280A kindred24,25. Not only did we confirm the R136S mutation impairs LDLR binding28, besides HSPG binding, but we also showed reduced LDLR-mediated uptake of ApoE-associated lipid cargoes and decreased lipofuscinosis. Decreased LDLR binding may thus also contribute to the protective effects of this mutation26,27.

Studies suggest poorly lipidated ApoE may seed Aβ due to its propensity to aggregate and that lipidated ApoE is protective in AD models1. Our work suggests an enrichment of PUFAs, such as PUFA-CEs, in the ApoE particle may render lipidated ApoE pathological, particularly in the aging brain where oxidative stress is greater and the degradative capacity of lysosomes is diminished. Since PUFA-CE is strongly upregulated in the brain of amyloid mouse models14, Aβ elevation may significantly amplify this pathogenic process as part of the amyloid cascade. Although ApoE isoform-dependent receptor binding could only explain protective mechanism of ApoE2, with ApoE4 minimally differentiated from ApoE3 in most uptake assays, higher aggregation propensity of lipApoE4 compared to lipApoE3 shown in our study may contribute to its exacerbated pathogenicity over ApoE3 upon internalization. Interestingly, while lipApoE2 is aggregation-prone at acidic pH in the cell-free assay, its reduced internalization ability may protect it against this potentially deleterious structural property, which has been revealed in other settings73. This may explain why APOE2 is a risk factor for age-related macular degeneration, since drusen, the autofluorescent lipid particles that are prominent in the diseased eye, accumulate and likely oxidize extracellularly74. Collectively, our data suggest the ApoE allelic series, in the lipidated form, may modify LOAD risk via two distinct but related mechanisms: (i) a protective mechanism mediated by decreased lipApoE-receptor interactions, as shown for ApoE2 and Christchurch; and (ii) a deleterious mechanism mediated by increased aggregation of PUFA-containing lipoproteins upon reaching the acidic lysosomes, as exhibited by lipApoE4.

Our study suggests unchecked endolysosomal delivery of ApoE and its lipid cargoes, including PUFA-CE, is pathogenic and that inhibiting lipApoE-receptor interactions in the CNS may reduce LOAD risk or disease progression, in part by decreasing formation of lipofuscin and promoting lysosomal health. Rescue of lipApoE4-induced phenotypes by mutations impairing receptor interactions indicates ApoE4 can be modified to phenotypically resemble protective variants, potentially paving the way for development of novel therapeutics.

## LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY
In this study, we used artificially lipidated ApoE containing only 1–2 lipid species, which does not capture the complexity of brain ApoE lipoproteins. Our study focuses on ApoE isoform-dependent effects downstream of ApoE-LDLR interaction without addressing the source of lipApoE in the brain (e.g., astrocytes or microglia). While our study showed a key role of LDLR in the uptake of ApoE and associated lipids, we cannot rule out contributions of other ApoE receptors to isoform-dependent phenotypes. Lastly, our study suggests lipofuscin induced by PUFA-CE lipApoE may promote pathological tau accumulation in cell culture, but it did not directly test this in vivo.

#### METHOD DETAILS
###### Expression and purification of HEK cell-derived ApoE
A DNA fragment encoding for full length (including signal sequence) human ApoE3 protein was synthesized with codon optimization for mammalian expression and cloned into a pcDNA3.1 vector (GenScript). Mutagenesis of the resulting construct was performed to generate the following constructs: ApoE2, R136S ApoE3, ApoE4, R136S ApoE4, and K146E ApoE4. Proteins were expressed by transient transfection in HEK293 cells at 37°C for 5 days. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min, after which the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter and loaded onto a HiTrap Heparin HP column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT and 10 mM sodium cholate (Buffer A). The column was then washed with 18 column volumes (CV) of 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% Triton X-100, followed by an additional 18 CV of Buffer A. Bound protein was eluted with a 20 CV gradient to 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 10 mM sodium cholate. Eluted fractions containing purified ApoE were pooled, concentrated to >2 mg/ml and loaded onto a HiLoad Superdex S200 pg (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl and 1 mM TCEP. Fractions containing purified ApoE were again pooled and concentrated to >2 mg/ml. All ApoE preparations used in iMg experiments had endotoxin levels < 1 EU/mg.

To generate materials for the homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence (HTRF)-based ApoE-LDLR binding assay, constructs encoding for ApoE2, ApoE3 and ApoE4 were used as template. DNA fragments lacking the native signal sequence were subcloned into a derived pRK5 containing a mouse kappa light chain signal sequence followed by a 6x histidine (His) tag, a (GGGS)2 linker, a BirA ligase recognition sequence (Avi tag, GLNDIFEAQKIEWHE) and another (GGGS)2 linker. Proteins were expressed by transient transfection in HEK293 cells at 37°C for 5 days. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter and loaded onto a Ni-NTA Excel resin pre-equilibrated with 25 mM Tris pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT and 10 mM sodium cholate (Buffer B). The column was washed with 18 CV of 25 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, followed by 18 CV of Buffer B. Protein was eluted using a gradient of 25 mM Tris pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 500mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT and 10 mM sodium cholate. Elution fractions containing purified ApoE were pooled, concentrated to >2 mg/ml and resolved on a Superdex S200 16/60 column pre-equilibrated with 25 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl and 1 mM DTT. Fractions containing purified ApoE were pooled and concentrated to >2 mg/ml.

###### ApoE lipidation
Purified ApoE was lipidated with 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) using a previously described procedure80, with some modifications. A solution of POPC (Avanti Polar Lipids, #850457) in chloroform was dried under vacuum for 30 minutes using a Rotavap and further dried overnight under vacuum. The resulting film was resuspended to a final concentration of 10 mg/mL using a solution of 25 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl supplemented with 100mM sodium cholate (molar ratio of POPC:cholate = 1:8). Purified ApoE was mixed with the solution to the molar ratio of 1:130 for ApoE:POPC and incubated under constant rotation at RT. After 2 h, the reaction mixture was transferred to 4°C and an equal volume of bio-beads (Biobeads SM2 resin, BioRad) were added to remove excess lipids, with the beads replaced 3 times over the course of a 48 h period. Finally, the bio-beads were filtered out using 0.22 μm filter Eppendorf units and the resulting lipid-reconstituted ApoE nanodiscs were polished over a HiLoad Superdex S200 pg (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with PBS and 1 mM DTT, under low endotoxin conditions.

For CE(20:4)/POPC lipidation of ApoE for use in the lipofuscin assay, an aliquot of CE(20:4) (Cayman Chem, 22595) was freshly resuspended into chloroform and mixed with POPC at a molar ratio of 1:4 for CE(20:4):POPC. Lipid preparation and lipidation were carried out as described above for POPC lipidation. The earliest eluting peak from SEC purification (Figure S5B) was found to be the most efficient in lipofuscin induction on NGN2 iNeurons in pilot experiments and was thus selected for experiments shown in this study. CE(18:1)/POPC-lipidated ApoE4 was generated following the same protocol, except for switching CE(20:4) with CE(18:1) (Avanti Polar Lipids, #700269).

The concentrations of lipidated ApoE preparations were measured by NanoDrop Spectrophotometer and adjusted to the same concentrations for different isoforms. Aliquots of lipidated ApoE were snap frozen and stored at −80°C. Thawed aliquots were temporarily stored at 4°C and used within 3 weeks. CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE4 used for mouse brain injection had an endotoxin level < 10 EU/mg.

###### Negative stain TEM for lipidated ApoE
Samples were diluted to a final concentration of 2.5 to 5 μg/mL in 25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl; 3 μL of the solution was placed onto a CF2/1 300 grid from Electron Microscopy Sciences (EMS) previously cleaned using a PELCO EasiGlow glow system discharge (Ted Pella). After 60 seconds incubation, sample was blotted with Whatman paper and stained three times into 20 μL of a filtered solution of 1% uranyl acetate (30 seconds with last drop). Grids were imaged using a Hitachi7800 TEM with a NanoSprint15 Mk-II AMT camera (AMT Imaging). All image processing was performed using CryoSPARC81. Micrographs were contrast transfer function (CTF) corrected using CTFFIND482. Particles were then selected using the blob picker and subjected to several rounds of 2D classification.

###### Purification of recombinant human LDLR extracellular domain
The full-length human low density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) extracellular domain (ECD, residues A1-R767) with its native signal peptide sequence was synthesized (Genscript) with codon optimization for mammalian expression and cloned into a derived pRK5 vector upstream of a C-terminal Avi tag, followed by a TEV cleavage site (ENLYFQG) and a 6x His tag. Protein was expressed by transient transfection in HEK293 cells at 37°C for 5 days. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter and loaded onto Ni-NTA Excel resin pre-equilibrated with 25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl2 (Buffer C) at 4°C. The resin was washed with Buffer C supplemented with 35 mM imidazole and protein was then step eluted with Buffer C containing 300 mM imidazole. For cell-based assays, the elution fractions containing purified LDLR ECD were pooled, concentrated to >2 mg/ml and resolved on a HiLoad Superdex S200 column pre-equilibrated with Buffer C, under low endotoxin conditions. Fractions containing purified LDLR ECD were pooled and concentrated to >2 mg/ml. In cellular experiments, human LDLR ECD purchased from R&D Systems (2148-LD/CF-MTO) was used interchangeably with internally generated materials.

For HTRF experiments, elution fractions from the Ni-NTA affinity purification were pooled and incubated with TEV protease overnight while dialyzing against Buffer C at 4°C. Cleaved LDLR-ECD was then reloaded onto a Ni-NTA Excel column, washed with Buffer C supplemented with 30mM and eluted with Buffer C containing 300 mM imidazole. The flowthrough and wash fractions containing tag-cleaved LDLR-ECD were pooled and in-vitro biotinylated using a BirA Biotin-protein ligase biotinylation kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Avidity LLC). The reaction was incubated at RT for 3 h and the biotionlyated LDLR-ECD was separated from free biotin by resolving on a Superdex S200 16/60 column pre-equilibrated with Buffer C. Fractions containing purified, biotinylated LDLR ECD were pooled and concentrated to >2 mg/ml.

###### HTRF for lipApoE-LDLR binding assessment
Homogeneous Time Resolved Fluorescence (HTRF) measurements83 were carried out using an anti-6xHis-XL665 mAb acceptor and Streptavidin-Eu cryptate donor according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay was performed in a buffer consisting of 100 mM Tris HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, and 0.1% BSA. LDLR ECD-Biotin was dispensed into a 384-well assay plate (AlphaPlate 384-well, Perkin Elmer), in triplicate. Then, 8-point, 2-fold serial dilutions of His-tagged ApoE were prepared in assay buffer and dispensed into the 384-well assay plate in triplicate, followed immediately by sequential additions of 1X acceptor and donor fluorophores. Each well therefore contained 10 nM LDLR ECD-biotin, 0.3X each of acceptor and donor fluorophores, and the indicated concentrations of ApoE, in a total volume of 20 μL. The assay plate was then covered in adhesive foil to protect it from light and incubated for 1 hour at RT while shaking at 1050 rpm on an orbital plate shaker. After 1 hour, the plate was read on BMG Labtech PHERAstar, dual time-resolved fluorescence on endpoint mode with top optic. Measurements were made with Read A at 337/665 and Read B at 337/620. The assay signal was calculated with Read A over Read B, multiplied by a factor of 10,000 on the MARS software. EC50s were calculated on Graph Pad Prism 9 and curves were fitted with a 4-parameter sigmoidal curve. Three separate titrations were performed in triplicate for each ApoE isoform with points representing mean +/− standard deviation of the average of the triplicates from each titration. In the competitive HTRF assay to assess inhibition of lipApoE-LDLR binding by different ApoE variants, untagged, lipidated ApoE was mixed with 30 nM LDLR ECD-Biotin for 30 min at RT and then combined with 30 nM His-tagged lipApoE4, along with donor and acceptor fluorophores described above. The total reaction volume remained at 20 uL and all the other assay parameters remained constant. Three separate titrations were performed in triplicate for each lipApoE variant.

###### SPR for lipApoE-LDLR binding measurement
For surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments, a C1 chip (Cytiva BR100540) was used in the Biacore 8k (Cytiva) that had been amine coupled with neutravidin (Thermo 31000) to a density of approximately 700 RUs. The biotinylated LDLR ECD (2 μg/mL) was immobilized onto the neutravidin surface to a density from 330 to 360 RUs. Lipidated ApoE2, E3, E4 were injected over the SPR surface at a flow rate of 50 μL per min with association time of 60 seconds and a final dissociation time of 300 seconds. Concentrations tested were as follows: 0.5, 1, and 2 μM for lipidated ApoE2; 0.0156, 0.03125, and 0.0625 μM for lipidated ApoE3 and ApoE4. All data were analyzed in the Biacore Insight Evaluation software (version 4.0.8.20368) and the curves were fit using a 1:1 kinetic model.

###### HPLC-based heparin binding assay
HEK-derived ApoE was diluted to 1.85 mg/mL in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, after which 50 μg of the sample was injected onto a POROS™ Heparin 50 μm Column, 2.1 × 30 mm, 0.1 mL (Thermo Scientific™, 4333411) pre-equilibrated with 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 (Buffer A). The column was washed with 5 CV of Buffer A and eluted with 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 containing 2 M NaCl (Buffer B) at 0.08 M/min NaCl gradient.

###### Cell-based LDLR binding assay
POPC/cholesterol-lipidated ApoE was labeled using FluoReporter FITC protein labeling kit (Invitrogen, F6434) with excess dye removed by dialysis into PBS using Slide-A-Lyzer MINI dialysis device with 10kDa MWCO (ThermoFisher, #69570). Concentrations of the FITC-labeled different ApoE isoforms were equalized based on densitometry of protein bands on a 4–12% Bis-Tris gel (Invitrogen) stained by InstantBlue (Abcam, ab119211). After adding 1mM DTT, the FITC-lipApoE preparations were stored at 4°C for up to 2 weeks. HEK293T cells were plated at 25,000 cells/well in a PDL-coated 96-well plate (Corning Biocoat, 354640). One day after plating, cells were transfected with Flag tagged-human LDLR on pcDNA3.1 (GenScript) using Lipofectamine LTX-Plus (Invitrogen, A12621) in serum-free Opti-MEM (GIBCO) and switched back into complete medium at 6 h after transfection. Two days after transfection, cells were stained with NucBlue (Invitrogen, R37605) for 15 min at 37°C and then incubated with 10 μg/mL FITC-labeled lipidated ApoE (~294 nM) diluted in cold medium for 1 h at 4°C. After removing the treatment medium, cells were washed once with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (GIBCO, 14025092) and placed on cold Live Cell Imaging Solution (Invitrogen, A14291DJ) before live imaging. After live imaging of cell-bound FITC-lipApoE, cells were fixed and immunostained to confirm equivalent LDLR overexpression across treatment conditions.

###### pHrodo-lipApoE uptake assay
For assessing cellular uptake, lipidated ApoE was labeled using pHrodo-iFL green microscale labeling kit (ThermoFisher, P36015) at a dye:protein ratio of 7:1. Excess dye removal and concentration adjustment were performed as that for FITC-labeled lipApoE described above with 1 mM DTT added. Aliquots of pHrodo green-lipApoE were snap frozen and stored at −80°C. Thawed aliquots were kept at 4°C for up to 2 weeks. Cells were plated at appropriate densities (8,000–10,000 cells/well for H4, ~20,000 cells/well for HAP1 lines, 15,000–20,000 cells/well for APOE KO iAstrocytes) in a PDL-coated 96-well plate (PerkinElmer, or Corning Biocoat). The next day, cells were treated with 10 μg/mL pHrodo green-labeled lipidated ApoE (~294 nM) in serum-free Opti-MEM. To test how uptake of lipApoE is mediated by cell surface receptors, pHrodo green-lipApoE was pre-incubated with LDLR ECD (R&D Systems #2148-LD/CF-MTO, or generated as described above), low-molecular-weight heparin (Fisher Scientific, BP2524), or CD98hc ECD84 at 37°C for 30–40 min before being added to cells. Note that the final concentrations of the molecules in treatment medium were 20 μg/mL for LDLR ECD (~224 nM) and CD98hc ECD (~434 nM) and 2.5 μg/mL for heparin (~400 nM), but pre-incubation with pHrodo green-lipApoE was done at 2x or 4x concentrations. For all live imaging experiments, nuclear staining was done by incubation with NucBlue. Live imaging for pHrodo green signals was performed at 5–6 h or 1 d after treatment. The exact uptake efficiency of ApoE4 vs. ApoE3 varies across different lipidation batches, but an overall trend of ApoE4 >= ApoE3 > ApoE2 was observed.

###### pHrodo-HDL/BODIPY-CE uptake assays and CE(18:1)/HDL/ApoE treatment
High-density lipoprotein (HDL, EMD Milipore, LP3–5MG) was stored at 4°C for up to 2 months in Nitrogen gas-purged glass vials as small aliquots to minimize oxidation. BODIPY-CE (CholEsteryl BODIPY™ FL C12, ThermoFisher C3927MP) and CE(18:1) (Avanti Polar Lipids #700269) were reconstituted in chloroform to 1 mg/mL, aliquoted into glass vials at 500 μL or 1 mL per vial and dried under Nitrogen gas. Dried CE was kept at −20°C or −80°C for long-term storage or re-dissolved in ethanol to reach 1 mg/mL with the aid of a heat gun accompanied by vigorous vortexing. Small aliquots of CE in ethanol were kept at −20°C in Nitrogen gas-purged glass vials for short-term use within 1–2 months. Right before cell treatment, an aliquot of CE was warmed on a 37°C heat block for ~5 min followed by vigorous vortexing to ensure the powder was fully dissolved again.

For assessing ApoE-mediated HDL uptake, HDL was labeled using pHrodo-iFL red microscale labeling kit (ThermoFisher, P36014) or pHrodo-iFL green microscale labeling kit (ThermoFisher, P36015) following the same protocol as that for lipApoE labeling described above with 1 mM DTT added. Aliquots of pHrodo-labeled HDL were stored at 4°C and used within 2 weeks. One day after plating cells to a PDL-coated 96-well plate, pHrodo-labeled HDL was pre-complexed with non-lipidated ApoE with and without the addition of LDLR ECD or heparin at 37°C for ~45 min before being added to cells in serum-free Opti-MEM. The final concentrations in the treatment medium were 10 μg/mL for pHrodo-HDL and ApoE, 20 μg/mL for LDLR ECD, and 2.5 μg/mL for heparin, but the pre-complexing was typically done at 2x or 4x concentrations.

BODIPY-CE uptake assay or CE(18:1)/HDL/ApoE treatment was performed following a similar protocol as pHrodo-HDL uptake except with the use of unlabeled HDL and the inclusion of BODIPY-CE or CE(18:1) during pre-complexing. The final concentrations in the treatment medium were 10 μg/mL for ApoE, HDL and BODIPY-CE or CE(18:1). Note that extracellular BODIPY-CE fluorescence signals are extensively self-quenched, thus imaging for BODIPY-CE uptake was performed without medium removal as BODIPY signals only show up after cellular uptake of BODIPY-CE. For lipidomic study on CE(18:1)/HDL/ApoE-treated APOE KO iAstrocytes, ApoE4 treatment was done with and without 20 μg/mL LDLR ECD or 500nM ACAT1 inhibitor K60485.

For BODIPY-CE/HDL/ApoE treatment of APOE KO iMg, cells were plated onto a PDL-coated 96-well plate with additional coating of Vitronectin (ThermoFisher, A31804) and switched from Microglia Maturation Medium into NGD medium86 supplemented with 20 ng/mL of hM-CSF (Peprotech,300–25) and 20 ng/mL of hIL34 (Peprotech, 200–34), i.e., NGD++ medium. The plating density was at 8,000–12,000 cells/well for the first round of differentiation and 16,000–18,000 cells/well for the second round of differentiation. One day after plating, iMg were treated with 10 μg/mL ApoE, HDL and BODIPY-CE pre-complexed at 37°C for ~45 min at 2x concentrations in NGD++ medium with the addition of 100 ng/mL LPS. Live imaging of BODIPY signals was performed after 1 d incubation.

###### DiI-LDL uptake assay
Two paradigms were used to assess uptake of DiI-conjugated LDL (ThermoFisher, L3482) in H4 cells. In the co-treatment paradigm, cells were serum-starved in Opti-MEM for 1.5 – 2 h before being treated with 10 μg/mL DiI-LDL and 10 μg/mL POPC-lipidated ApoE simultaneously in Opti-MEM, with live imaging performed at 3–4 h after treatment. In the second paradigm, cells were pre-treated with 10 μg/mL POPC-lipidated ApoE in Opti-MEM for 1 d, followed by spike-in of DiI-LDL to reach 10 μg/mL and further incubation for 3–4 h before live imaging.

###### Lipofuscin induction in NGN2 iNeurons
To monitor lipofuscin formation over time, NGN2 iNeurons were treated with 20 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE in 50% fresh medium on day 7 and replenished with 10 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE in 50% fresh medium on days 14 and 21. Live imaging for autofluorescent lipofuscin was performed on days 14, 21 and 28 (i.e., after 7 d, 14 d and 21 d treatment, respectively). Lipofuscin was detected in a broad spectrum but appeared the most intense in the 488 nm channel, which was used for image capture and quantification. To confirm the role of LDLR on lipofuscin induction in iNeurons, live imaging for lipofuscin was performed after 3 d treatment of 20 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE4 with or without 20 μg/m LDLR ECD that was pre-incubated for 30 min at 37°C with ApoE. For lipidomic study, NGN2 iNeurons were infected with lentivirus expressing P301S 4R2N tau (gene synthesis into pLVX-puro vector by GenScript, lentiviral production by SignaGen) at MOI of 5 on day 11, treated with 20 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE in 50% fresh medium on day 14 and replenished with 10 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE in 50% fresh medium on day 21. Lipid extraction was done on day 28.

###### pHrodo-tau pff accumulation assay in NGN2 iNeurons
4R2N full-length tau carrying P301S mutation was expressed and purified by CepterBio as previously described87. For fibrillization, 40 μM P301S tau was incubated with 40 μM low-molecular-weight heparin and 2 mM DTT in PBS for 5 d at 37°C with constant agitation at 1,000 rpm in a ThermoMixer fitted with a heated lid. At the end of the incubation, ultracentrifugation was performed on the fibrillization reaction at 100,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, following which the supernatant was removed and the pellet fraction containing preformed tau fibrils (tau pffs) was resuspended in PBS. Proteins in the supernatant and the pellet fractions were run on a 4–12% Bis-Tris gel and stained with InstantBlue for estimating the concentration of tau in the pellet fraction. For assessing neuronal uptake and accumulation of tau pffs, the P301S tau pffs were diluted to 1 mg/mL and labeled using pHrodo-iFL red microscale labeling kit (ThermoFisher, P36014) at a dye:protein ratio of 8:1. Excess dye was removed by 2 rounds of ultracentrifugation with the resulting pellet resuspended in PBS with sonication, aliquoted and stored at −80°C. pHrodo-tau pff treatment was done on NGN2 iNeurons after 14 d incubation with 20 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE or CE(18:1)/POPC-lipidated ApoE, whereby 50% of conditioned medium was removed followed by the addition of sonicated pHrodo-tau pffs to a final concentration of 2.5 μg/mL. Live imaging of pHrodo-tau signals was performed after 13–14 d incubation, during which cells were replenished with 10 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE or CE(18:1)/POPC-lipidated ApoE once at 7 d after tau pff addition.

###### Lipofuscin induction in H4 and HAP1 cells
Lipofuscin induction on H4 and various lines of HAP1 cells (WT, LDLR KO, LRP1 KO) was monitored by live imaging after 3 d incubation with 20 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE or CE(18:1)/POPC-lipidated ApoE in serum-free Opti-MEM. To validate the role of LDLR in lipofuscin induction in H4 cells, 20 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE4 was added to cells with or without 20 μg/mL LDLR ECD that was pre-incubated for 30 min at 37°C with ApoE. To test modulation of lipofuscin accumulation by Vitamin E, 20 μg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE3 was pre-incubated with 25 μM Vitamin E (Sigma,V-082–1ML, in methanol) or volume-matched methanol (vehicle control) for 30 min at 37°C before adding the mixture to H4 cells for 1 d incubation. To test the effect of Lalistat 2 (Cayman, 25347–5) on lipofuscin accumulation, H4 cells were pre-treated with 25 μM Lalistat 2 (in DMSO) or volume-matched DMSO (vehicle control) for 1 d before the spike-in of CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE3 to a final concentration of 20 μg/mL followed by 1 d further incubation.

###### Cathepsin activity assay
6QC probe was synthesized as previously described40 and reconstituted by DMSO into 5 mM stock solution. For measuring cathepsin activity in H4 cells pre-treated with ApoE, 6QC probe was diluted into 5 μM in serum-free Opti-MEM together with NucBlue and incubated with H4 cells for 2 h at 37°C before live imaging. Upon cleavage by lysosomal cathepsins, the 6QC probe becomes de-quenched and emits fluorescence in the 647nm channel.

###### Immunocytochemistry
Immunostaining for cell surface LDLR was performed by incubating live cells with an anti-human LDLR antibody (R&D Systems, AF2148) diluted 1:200 in live staining medium (cold Opti-MEM supplemented with 25 mM HEPES) for 1 h at 4°C. After three washes with cold live staining medium, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at RT. After two PBS washes, cells with blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at RT, followed by incubation with donkey anti-goat Alexa 647nm secondary antibody (Invitrogen) diluted 1:1000 in 5% BSA for 2 h at RT. Following two PBS washes, cells were incubation with DAPI (Invitrogen, D1306) diluted 1:5000 in PBS for at least 15 min. Imaging was done after one last PBS wash. For immunostaining of intracellular proteins, including internalized lipApoE, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min at RT, washed twice with PBS, blocked with 5% BSA containing 0.05% saponin for 1 h at RT and incubated with primary antibodies (see Key Resources Table) diluted in 5% BSA containing 0.05% saponin for 2–3 h at RT or overnight at 4°C. After removal of primary antibody staining solution, cells were washed 3x in PBS and incubated with Alexa-labeled secondary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA containing 0.05% saponin for 1–2 h at RT. Post-secondary PBS washes and DAPI staining were done as that for cell surface LDLR staining described above. For labeling of recycling endosomes, cells were incubated with Alexa 488nm-conjugated transferrin for 15 min before fixing by 4% PFA. To confirm perfect colocalization of internalized ApoE with LDLR was not due to bleed-through, ApoE staining was done with and without addition of the primary antibody for human LDLR but with both secondary antibodies, which showed completely no signals in the channel corresponding to LDLR in the absence of primary antibody for LDLR.

###### High-content imaging and image analysis
Live imaging or imaging of fixed and immunostained cells was performed using Opera Phenix high-content imaging system (Revvity) with 40x or 63x water objective at the confocal mode. Fluorescent signals were automatically quantified using the spot analysis algorithm within Harmony HCS software (Revvity) with appropriate thresholding. Key parameters that were quantified include cell numbers, corrected spot intensity mean, total spot area and total number of spots. For most experiments, phenotypic comparisons were made based on the calculation of corrected spot intensity mean × total spot area / cell number (i.e., total signals/cell). For analysis of pHrodo-tau pff signals in subpopulation of iNeurons, perinuclear signals were quantified for neurons with > 3 lipofuscin spots per cell (arbitrarily defined as “lipofuscin-positive”). To make sure 4-HNE immunoreactivities were not contaminated by autofluorescent signals from lipofuscin, no primary antibody control was performed, which confirmed no detectable autofluorescent signals at the contrast set to visualize 4-HNE puncta which are much brighter. To analyze lysosomal clustering, LAMP1 spot analysis was performed separately with the splitting sensitivity set at 0 (minimum) or 1 (maximum). At the minimal splitting sensitivity, LAMP1 spots that are clustered together are automatically counted as a single spot. Subsequently, LAMP1 spot area covered by large single spots reaching a defined size threshold (“clustered” lysosomes) was expressed as a percentage of total LAMP1 spot area quantified using maximal splitting sensitivity (total lysosomes). The percentage of clustered/total lysosomes for each treatment condition was further normalized to the mean percentage across all conditions in each independent experiment and termed “LAMP1 clustering index”. For cell count estimation of APOE KO iMg before harvesting media for cytokine/chemokine assay, Digital Phase Contrast and Brightfield images were taken at 10x.

###### Super-resolution imaging and analysis
Fixed and stained cells in 96-well plate were rinsed twice with PBS prior to imaging on a Leica SP8 microscope using a 63x/1.4 N.A. oil immersion objective with a pixel size of ~30 nm at 63X magnification in lightning acquisition mode. For acquisition of super-resolution images, lightning processing was performed in the adaptive mode and images were then visualized and processed in LAS X and ImageJ. For colocalization analysis, ImageJ’s Coloc2 tool was used for calculating the Mander’s overlap coefficients.

###### Cell-free aggregation of lipidated ApoE
POPC-lipidated ApoE, CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE, and CE(18:1)/POPC-lipidated ApoE4 in PBS (pH 7.4) were diluted into 0.5 mg/mL using PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37°C for 4 d. To adjust the pH to 4.5, a pre-determined volume of diluted hydrochloric acid was spiked in at 1/10 of the total volume prior to 37°C incubation. To monitor the extent of protein aggregation, ultracentrifugation was performed by spinning the samples at 150,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, followed by separation of the supernatant and the pellet fraction. The pellet fraction was resuspended in the same volume of PBS as the starting sample. Both supernatant and pellet fractions were heated with sample buffer in the absence of reducing agent for 5 min at 95°C, run on a 4–12% Bis-Tris gel (Invitrogen) and stained by InstantBlue. Imaging of the gel and the densitometry analysis were performed using the Odyssey DLx Imaging System with Image Studio software (Li-Cor).

###### Lipofuscin imaging and quantifications for mouse brain tissues
For lipofuscin imaging of mouse brains shown in Figure 6A–E and Figure S6A–B, brain sections were from a previously published cohort10. ~50 μm brain sections were washed in PBS 3× 5 min, permeabilized in PBS containing 0.25% Triton X-100 for 30 min, incubated for 1hr in blocking buffer (2% donkey serum in 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS) and then stained overnight with anti-NeuN (1:500, ab177487) and anti-Iba1 (1:500, Novus Biologicals, NB100–1028) in blocking buffer. The next day, the sections were washed 3× 5 min in PBS, and incubated with secondary antibodies (donkey anti-Rb 488, Thermofisher A-21206; donkey anti-goat 647, Thermofisher A-21447) at room temperature for 1 h in dark. After 3× 5 min PBS washes, the tissues were stained with DAPI (Invitrogen D1306, 1:10,000 dilution in PBS) for 20 min in dark, washed 3× 5 min in PBS, and mounted on glass with ProlongGold Antifade media and scanned at 20x/0.75 N.A. using Leica Stellaris 8 Confocal microscope. Autofluorescent signals in the entire hippocampus and entorhinal cortex were quantified in the 545 nm excitation/600–650 nm emission channel using ImageJ software with different thresholding of signal over background. The number of DAPI-positive nuclei was calculated using “ImageJ > Analyze Particles > Add to ROI manager”; the levels of autofluorescent lipofuscin signals were then normalized to the number of nuclei. Representative images were acquired using a 40x/1.3 N.A. oil immersion objective.

###### Lipofuscin induction with in vivo injections of CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE4
For hippocampal injections of CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE4 or PBS into C57BL/6J mice, the surgical area was sterilized with 70% isopropanol and 4% chlorhexidine and surgical instruments were autoclaved. Mice were brought under anesthesia with 4% isoflurane. Hair was shaved from the base of the skull region to between the eyes prior to placement on the stereotaxic surface. 5 mg/kg Meloxicam, 3.25 mg/kg Ethiqa XR and 10 uL of 0.125% Bupivicaine were administered subcutaneously prior to any surgical procedures. With a maintenance level of ~1.5–2% isoflurane, a 1.5–2 mm incision was made in the center of the skull. Following application of 3% hydrogen peroxide, bregma and lambda were used as reference points to set coordinates and drill into the skull. Bilateral hippocampal injections were conducted for each animal at the following coordinates: AP −2.54, ML −2.98, DV 2.83 (PBS injection) and AP −2.54, ML +2.98, DV 2.83 (CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE4 injection), from brain surface. The injection needle (30GA, 15mm, 45 degree angle), attached to a 10μL Hamilton syringe (Hamilton 1701 RN) was inserted into the brain at a rate of 0.2 mm per second. After waiting for one minute to allow for brain sealing around the needle, 2.5 uL of PBS or 4 mg/mL CE(20:4)/POPC-lipidated ApoE4 in 2 mM DTT PBS was administered at 0.4 μL per minute. Five minutes following the injection, the needle was raised slowly out of the brain, the incision was sutured and mice were transferred to a cage placed over a heated recovery pad and observed for full recovery. Mice were monitored daily after surgery to check for weight loss, pain, discomfort, or infections.

Animals were sacrificed 14 d post-surgery. For terminal sample collection, animals were deeply anesthetized via IP injection of 2.5% Avertin (Millipore Sigma T48402–5G) prior to exsanguination. Animals were then transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS using a peristaltic pump (Gilson Inc. Minipuls Evolution). The brain was removed and post-fixed 24 h in 4% PFA at 4°C, after which tissue was cryoprotected in 30% sucrose and sliced coronally at 25 μm on a freezing microtome at and around the anterior-posterior level of injection. Free-floating sections were first washed in PBS, permeabilized for 15 min in 0.3% TritonX-100 in PBS, and then blocked for 1 h in a PBS based-buffer containing 1% BSA, 0.1% fish gelatin (BioWorld 21761058) and 0.5% TritonX-100. After blocking, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-NeuN antibody (1:500, Abcam, ab177487) and goat anti-Iba1 antibody (1:500, Novus, NB100–1028) in antibody buffer (1% BSA, 0.3% TritonX-100 in PBS) for 3 d at 4°C. Sections were then washed 3× 15 min in PBS + 0.05% Tween 20, before being incubated with secondary antibodies and DAPI in antibody buffer for 3 h at RT. Finally, stained tissues were washed in PBS + 0.05% Tween 20 before being mounted onto slides, dried, and then coverslipped with Prolong Glass mounting medium (Invitrogen, P36984).

For brain-wide lipofuscin imaging and intensity measurement, stained sections were imaged at 20x magnification using a widefield epifluorescence slide scanner (Axio Scan Z1; Carl Zeiss; 20x/0.8 N.A. air objective, with Zeiss Zen 3.7 software). Each channel was acquired independently with the 450–490 nm filter set for NeuN/488, the 625–655 nm filter set for Iba1/647 and the 538–562 nm filter set for capturing the lipofuscin (LF) autofluorescence, corresponding to emission wavelengths of 570–640 nm. Channel excitation intensities and shutter speeds, as well as other parameters, were kept consistent throughout the imaging run. One section per brain hemisphere most closely capturing the site of injection was quantified for LF content, using Zeiss Zen 3.7 software and custom macros written in Zen using Advanced Processing and Macros tools. First, automatic shading correction was applied and images were stitched together in Zen 3.7, then PBS and ApoE-injected hemispheres were denoted manually, and finally custom macros were used to apply consistent local thresholding to images as well as to identify and quantify both the total tissue area and LF-stained areas within each hemisphere. Total areas and total 555nm sum intensities were then quantified for both hemibrain tissue and LF objects, which were then summed across all objects per animal and hemisphere. These numbers were normalized to total hemisphere tissue area and used to compare LF content between conditions.

Confocal imaging was performed for quantifying cell type-specific LF content. For each animal, two regions were selected in both the hippocampus and the cortex to generate mean statistics. Regions were chosen to be close to the site of injection but not in the areas with high microgliosis or visible cell damage. Regions were also specifically selected to contain detectable levels of LF in both neurons and microglia, to facilitate cell-type analyses. Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica SP8; Leica Microsystems, Inc) with a 40x/1.3 N.A. oil objective, at a pixel size of 126 nm and a format size of 1024×1024. Confocal z-stacks of 0.5 μm were acquired sequentially for each channel, scanned at 1000 Hz between frames with a 2-frame average. Laser excitation and detection intensities and wavelengths were kept consistent throughout experiment, with NeuN-488 excited at 488 nm (0.4%) and collected at 495–555 nm, Iba1–647 excited at 638 nm (0.3%) and collected at 645–749nm, and LF autofluorescence excited at 552 nm (0.5%) and collected at 557–641nm. The representative image is a maximum projection of the three-dimensional image volume, generated using Imaris 10.1.1 (Oxford Instruments, Bitplane). For quantifying the intracellular levels of LF, the signal within the intraneuronal compartment was masked using an intensity-based segmentation of NeuN-positive pixels, and the resulting sum intensities were normalized to the total neuronal volume within a given 3D image volume. Intra-microglial LF was similarly masked using an intensity-based segmentation of Iba1-positive pixels, and the sum intensities of LF signal were quantified and normalized to the total microglial volume. In each case, the mean sum intensity was determined for each region selected (hippocampus and cortex), and these means were then used to calculate the volume-normalized intracellular LF signals.

###### RNA isolation and qPCR
Cells were lysed with Qiagen RLT-plus buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol, snap frozen and store at −80°C. RNA was extracted from cell lysates using the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen, 74034), followed by cDNA generation by reverse transcription (ThermoFisher VILO Superscript IV #11756050). Transcript levels were evaluated using various TaqMan probes for specific genes of interest (refer to Key Resources Table) with expression of each gene normalized to the housekeeping gene ACTB (Thermofisher, Hs01060665_g1). qPCR was performed using a QuantStudio 6 flex system (Applied Biosystems) and average CT values were collected for each probe between technical duplicates. Delta CT values for each sample from the housekeeping gene were calculated, and delta delta CT values relative to samples from the control condition were calculated to generate relative expression for each gene of interest.

###### Bulk transcriptome sequencing & analysis for APOE KO iMg
At the end of each independent APOE KO iMg experiment, cells were lysed with Qiagen RLT-plus buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol. Samples were stored at −80°C until cell lysates from all individual experiments were collected, at which point, all lysates were thawed with technical triplicates for each condition in each experiment pooled into a single sample. ERRC control RNA that was pre-diluted at 1:10,000 was spiked into each sample at a volume directly proportional to the cell count determined during imaging. RNA extraction was performed using the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen, 74034). Quality control of RNA samples was performed using the Agilent 2200 TapeStation System to determine RNA concentration and quality. RNA samples were diluted 1:10 in nuclease-free water prior to loading on the TapeStation System. High sensitivity RNA ScreenTape (Agilent, 5067–5579) was loaded onto the system for sample injections and high sensitivity RNA ScreenTape Ladder (Agilent, 5067–5581) was loaded onto the system as a standard.

For each of the 12 independent biological samples, two replicate sequencing libraries were prepared from 10–60 ng of total RNA each using the QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit FWD for Illumina (Lexogen A01173). The UMI second strand synthesis module was used to introduce unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), following the protocol defined by the manufacturer. Briefly, oligo dT primers were hybridized to total RNA for reverse transcription, followed by RNA removal after first strand synthesis. UMIs were introduced during second strand synthesis and cDNA was purified using magnetic beads, followed by 18 cycles of PCR with dual indexes and PCR purification. Library quantity and quality were assessed with the High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent 5067–5584). Libraries were pooled in equimolar ratios and the sequencing pool was purified with magnetic beads to remove residual adaptor dimers. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument (75 bp single end) by SeqMatic (Fremont, CA, USA).

Sequencing data was processed using nf-core/rnaseq (v3.11.2) of the nf-core collection of workflows88, using STAR (v2.7.9a)89 and salmon (v1.10.1)90 for alignment of reads to the GRCh38 human reference genome and quantification of Gencode (v42) gene models, respectively. One library failed QC (fewer than 1 million total counts) and was excluded from downstream analysis. Differential expression analysis was performed with the edgeR (v3.42.4)91 and limma (v 3.56.2)92 Bioconductor packages using R v4.3.1. In brief, count matrices were subset to protein-coding genes, and library size factors were calculated with the TMM method93. Gene-wise linear models with experimental group and collection batch as covariates were fit with the voomLmFit function, setting the sample weights argument to TRUE. The relationships of technical and biological replicates were specified via the block argument. Fold changes and p-values for the contrasts of interest were obtained with the eBayes function, and false discovery rates were calculated according to previously published methods94. Gene set enrichment analyses were performed with the fgsea R package (v1.26.0)95 with gene set collections from the msigdbr package (v7.5.1). Volcano plots were generated with the ggplot2 R package (v3.4.3). Raw and processed RNA-seq data are available from the NCBI GEO repository under accession GSE250249.

###### Cytokine/chemokine assay for APOE KO iMg
After 1 d treatment with BODIPY-CE/HDL/ApoE complex described earlier, conditioned media collected from iMg were transferred to a 96-well V-bottom plate (Greiner, 651201), centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the resulting supernatants were transferred to PCR tubes (Thermofisher, AB2005) which were snap frozen and stored at −80 °C. After conditioned media from all four independent experiments were harvested, samples were sent to Eve Technologies (Canada) for cytokine/chemokine measurement using Human Cytokine/Chemokine Panel A 48-Plex Discovery Assay Array. Concentrations of cytokines/chemokines were normalized to cell count of each sample.

###### Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis of lipids
For lipid extraction from cells in 96-well plates, cells were washed with cold saline after complete removal of medium, followed by the addition of 100 μL/well of lipid extraction buffer consisting of 3:1 ratio of MS-grade butanol:methanol (v:v) with lipid standards added. After extensive scraping of the wells with pipette tips, the plate was sealed tightly and agitated in a plate shaker at 1,000 rpm for 25 min at 4°C. Next, the plate was incubated at −20°C for at least 1 h to facilitate protein precipitation, following which, the extracts were transferred to a v-bottom 96-well plate and centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. The top 70 μL supernatant from each well was collected with samples from technical replicates pooled into one glass vial. Samples were dried under Nitrogen gas and stored at −80°C. Right before liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LCMS) analysis, samples were resuspended in methanol and agitated on a shaker for ~15 min. Lipid extraction from CSF was performed as previously described96.

Lipid analyses for both cells and CSF samples were performed as descried in the same study96 by liquid chromatography (UHPLC Nexera X2) coupled to electrospray mass spectrometry (QTRAP 6500+). Lipids were analyzed in both positive and negative mode, using an UPLC BEH C18 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm particle size, Waters Corp.), maintained at 55°C, with gradient elution, and flow rate of 0.25 mL/min over 10 min gradient. The ion pair information, retention time and secondary spectrum data of the detected substances were collected for qualitative analysis. The multiple reaction monitoring mode (MRM) of triple quadrupole mass spectrometry was performed for semi-quantitative analysis. MRM data analysis was performed using Analyst 1.6.3 software (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) and MultiQuant (AB SCIEX). For peak area determinations, the data of all metabolites were extracted, and the individual area of the same metabolite was normalized to the integrated area of all peaks (Area Ratio). The quality control (QC) was mixed with all sample extracts.

###### Evaluation of gene expression levels of APOE receptors in the human brain
Expression of APOE receptors across major brain cell types was explored using human single-nucleus RNA-seq data generated from the postmortem brain tissue provided by the ROSMAP (Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Project) cohort at Rush Alzheimer’s Disease Center, Rush University Medical Center, Chicago97. Results published here are in whole or in part based on data obtained from the AD Knowledge Portal (https://adknowledgeportal.org). Data from the study are available vis Synapse (https://www.synapse.org/#!Synapse:syn31512863). FASTQ files were mapped to the human genome (GRCh38) using cellranger (v7.0.0). All subsequent analysis was performed using R (v4.3). Resulting single-cell libraries were processed using Seurat v498 and the scran suite of tools99. Nuclei were retained if they contained expression of at least 200 unique genes and at least 1000 total gene counts. Nuclei were removed if >10% of their gene expression was mitochondrial. Doublets were predicted using the scDblFinder package and subsequently removed100. The final dataset consisted of 391 donors. Broad cell type labels were predicted with the SingleR package using the Allen Brain Atlas human brain data set as a reference101. Final cell type labels were refined using manual clustering and marker gene analysis in a small subset of donor datasets. These final labels were then projected onto the remaining donor data sets using the label transfer pipeline in Seurat v4. Finally, pseudobulk libraries were generated by aggregating counts across cell types and donor IDs using the scuttle package102. Gene expression is represented as log2 normalized values using a prior pseudocount of 1.

###### Quantification and Statistical analysis
For each independent experiment, readouts from 2–4 technical replicates were first averaged for each treatment group and then normalized to the mean or median across treatment groups in that experiment. Normalization to median was the default method, but normalization to mean was used in some experiments to avoid a loss of error bar for one of the experimental conditions that consistently emerges as the median. Normalized data from 3–4 independent experiments were combined for statistical analysis in Prism. In all graphs, data represent mean ± SEM with each dot in bar graphs representing one independent experiment or one animal. One-way or two-way ANOVA was performed followed by multiple comparisons using specific correction methods stated in the Figure Legends. Significant differences were only plotted for comparisons among ApoE isoforms in graphs unless otherwise indicated. Statistical significance was noted in individual graphs as follows: ns for p > 0.05, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. For analysis of cellular lipidomic data from APOE KO iAstrocytes and NGN2 iNeurons, area ratio for each lipid species was first normalized to the sum of all detected lipid species in that sample (i.e., input normalization) before further normalization to the mean of that particular lipid species within each independent experiment followed by log transformation. Global analysis of differences in lipid concentrations among treatment groups was performed using the online version of MetaboAnalyst 5.0103. Heatmaps for top lipid species different among treatment conditions were automatically generated by MetaboAnalyst 5.0 with lipid species ranked by fold of changes. The heatmap shown in Figure 3F also included unsupervised clustering of datasets automatically generated by the online tool. Statistical analysis for the most significantly altered lipid species based on MetaboAnalyst analysis was subsequently performed in Prism with one-way ANOVA followed by Holm-Sidak’s multiple comparisons. For analysis of lipidomic data from H4 cells with and without Lalistat 2 treatment, unknown variations in data unrelated to biological factors of interest were removed using the ruv package (version 0.9.7.1). Analysis of human CSF lipids was performed using R statistical software. Area ratio values were transformed using a log base 2 transformation. Differences between groups were estimated using a linear mixed-effects model, with subjects treated as random effects to address the repeated measurements. To account for the lack of balance in age and sex across groups, both covariates were adjusted as linear effects across all analytes. A mixed effects model adjusting for age*gender is used here to account for longitudinal measures with the same subject. For microscopy, representative images were included in the figures, based on quantifications.
